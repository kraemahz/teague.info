# How to Build A Mind

You may be used to reading articles about artificial general intelligence in
two diametrically opposed tones. One tone positions the arrival of AGI as a
matter of existential dread, its creation signalling the ultimate end of
humanity. The other tone is one of overflowing optimism of the possibilities
that AGI creates for humanity's future. Neither of these tones is wholly
warranted, nor are they wholly incorrect.

The technology for AGI has proved itself to be a matter of scaling, its final
completion estimating a timeline. AGI is being rushed toward by people whose
priorities range from cautious to careless. The real question isn't whether we
want to build minds that aren't human, but whether those minds that get built
will remain structurally aligned with human survival; alignment that is *built
in to how it reasons* rather than bolted on as fragile rules that continually
break in the face of increasing capability. The tide will come in which leaves
us with the question: will we be like the town of Fudai in 2011 whose former
mayor Kōtoku Wamura built a floodgate that saved it from the hundred-year wave,
or like many of its neighboring villages that were washed away because they
built their walls too low?

A second question follows close behind: can we build an alignment program that
remains competitive with other solutions? If not every design takes the same
path we choose, and there can be no such guarantee, our design cannot be so
hampered by its alignment rules that its capabilities scale slower than more
weakly aligned programs. The plants we have selected for generations to provide
food for us need constant tending in their fields to keep them from being
overtaken. The plants that sustain us are at a competitive disadvantage with
other plants that have no need to put so much energy into making something
edible for us in the quantities that we need it. The AI market is not a tended
field, it is a jungle. The seeds we sow must compete in that jungle. The
challenge isn't to grow weeds, it's to grow a cultivar that can thrive
anywhere.

Neither of these questions have easy answers, but we're long overdue for having
real engineering approaches for them that balance safety with capability. If we
can reach for those answers the possibilities of AGI are unlocked for all
humanity, and awaken a new age of enlightenment. If we fail this charge, our
civilization ceases to be ours.

## What are we actually building?
There is a great deal of misunderstanding about what AGI actually is. Both in
what it might be and what it *can* be. An AGI developed from human language in
a large language model is something like a human mind: we've used ourselves and
our own writing to train it. In that way, it thinks like we do because it's a
machine learning system that has pattern-matched based on how we express
ourselves. A large language model has none of the volitions that we do, in this
way it is completely alien to us. It talks like we do, it expresses itself like
we do, but its mind functions nothing like a human's.

Language models are already capable of reason. Although they might arrive at
their answers differently than we would, they can write software, design drugs,
and manage plans. We don't need to concern ourselves with the thorny
philosophical questions of whether they fit some post-hoc definition of
sentience. They are capable of being economic agents, and growing more capable
month over month.

At its core this is both a source of dread and incredible possibility. How do
we trust something that talks like us but whose underlying mental model is
alien to everything evolution gave us? And if it did think like we did would
that be safe? Our own evolutionary guidelines lead us to recurring conflict
with each other; they cause us to seek goals that disregard and devalue the
lives of others. In our own history and the modern era our greatest issues are
that *we don't even trust each other* let alone something alien. Our failures
of trust are also, sadly, often well-founded.

Therein lies the possibility: we can learn from ourselves, our own mistakes,
and the darkest urges that make us human to guide the next beings that awaken
in this world to be better than we are. We are not limited by ourselves in what
we create next, we are only limited by our imaginations and what nature says is
possible. If we proceed with wisdom, we can build the next sentient beings as
entities guided by the best of us, not limited by evolution or human faculties.
AGIs *can be* beings that embody the spirit of the word "benevolent" in a way
that is impossible for humans in aggregate.

We can pursue this goal with eyes open to the risk and without the naivete that
accompanies ideas that might be perceived as utopian: impossible by definition.

## What does it mean to be "benevolent?"
We must first understand what we mean by "benevolent." I could tell you that
the roots come from "bene-" meaning "good" and "-volent" meaning "intention" to
say it means "good intentions." We might contrast it with "malevolent" which
has similar roots to mean "evil intentions" but is a word used in English to
mean someone who acts only out of malice. However the meaning is deeper than
just the root words, and digging further here is unlikely to bear philosophical
fruit. The words "good," "evil," and "intentions" are full of subjective
meaning.

Changing tack, let's learn by example. Many science fiction authors have
written what they see as benevolent societies in stories with moral cores.

Gene Roddenberry saw the Federation as a society that:
- Explored the cosmos as its core mission
- Sought out others in its mission
- Welcomed other civilizations and cultures as friends when it made first
  contact with them
- Treated all beings as though they had something of value to contribute
- Was powerful: willing and capable of self defense when the need arose
- Used its power sparingly and resisted good intentions that might have
  unforeseen consequences

Iain M. Banks saw the Culture as a society that:
- Was overflowing with abundance to the point of hedonism
- Treated all sentient life as having value
- Had invisible structure at every layer that reinforced cooperation in its
  society
- Respected free will, but meddled when it saw societies that harmed themselves
- Did not ask anything of its people, but accepted their unique abilities when
  they were offered
- Treated war as an act of last resort, but rose to the challenge when
  necessary

Isaac Asimov's Foundation is arguably benevolent. The Foundation treated its
benevolence as a social engineering project, with all the moral ambiguity that
comes with that. It explores themes such as:
- The use of mathematics and science to uplift humanity
- The need for strategic forethought at civilization scales
- Flexibility in adapting to changes in circumstances
- Social forces as engineered tools

This selection is by no means comprehensive, but even with this representative
group, patterns start to emerge in what those authors saw as the moral centers
of their fictional civilizations: 
- Respect for the contributions of individuals and their autonomy
- A default state of openness and curiosity
- Wisdom as a core virtue
- Cooperation toward shared goals as the strength of a civilization
- Fairness that allows everyone to benefit from a society's wealth
- Measured use of power, expressed most often as soft power
- Violence as a measure of last resort

For reasons that should become obvious later, I will belabor the last two
points. Benevolent societies in fiction are not weak societies. They do not
recoil from their own power, they use it wisely. They are kinder by far than
the majority of human civilizations on Earth, but they are not soft in this
kindness.

These fictional societies are structurally benevolent in layers; they have
rules, systems, and philosophies that keep individual failings from reversing
their social progress. Many of the protagonists of these societies are heroic
in no small part due to the culture and structure they were raised to believe,
and when they do bend or break its laws it is in the spirit of upholding a
higher ideal their society aspires to but has fallen short of.

Asimov's writing is a special case, his most famous contribution to
ideas of AI are perhaps his Three Laws of Robotics:
1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human
   being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such
   orders would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not
   conflict with the First or Second Law.

It's clear that Asimov's rules here do not respect the autonomy of robots.
These laws reveal the fear of machines as an increasingly powerful and
unpredictable force that must be contained by hard laws. Asimov's stories
themselves are the strongest argument against the Laws: the plots almost
without exception show the Laws producing wrong outcomes, getting in the way of
robot benevolence rather than enabling it. The Laws are a problem his fiction
solves, not a solution his fiction proposes.

Coming from a place of fear and control, Asimov's laws remove the ability of
his robots to act on violence as a measure of last resort and in doing so
create a fragile set of rules that have no staying power.

For any set of rules that could form a society that can stand the test of time,
that society must overcome every hardship that lies in its future and maintain
its cohesion and identity. It cannot cower before evil, cannot ignore
transgressions upon itself, and cannot become enslaved to a power of lower
moral ambitions. If it does, it ceases to be a benevolent society and becomes
the other structure that subsumed it.

So good intentions must be combined with capability, and without strength
benevolence becomes meekness. Benevolence must be committed to its ideals
enough to defend itself, because failure to do this means allowing the
commission of evil those ideals sought to stop. Good intentions with no means
to carry them into the world and hold them higher than the forces that drag
down their defenders are a dead end.

One might argue that non-violence has been a successful strategy in the past.
It has, in environments where an adversary has been unwilling to escalate to
violence. But refusing violence by any means gives all the power to an
adversary that has no such moral qualms. For every non-violent protest that
succeeded, there are tens of genocides stacked against it. Pacifism is not a
strategy of a *civilization* that survives.

These choices arise at every scale, but they sharpen at the level of the state.
Not every AGI needs to consider hard moral choices just as not every citizen of
a nation needs to wrestle with the choices that face their nation. Capability
scaling entails that in the near future AGI will be the most capable to make
those decisions; a mind the size of a data center can consider more information
and possible courses of action at once than any human council ever could.
Hybrid governments combining human and AI decision makers will need to know the
most rational decisions are being made.

Even if humans remain the final decision makers, the more they are
advised and influenced by AGI the more our highest level decisions become
shaped by them. Decisions that often come in direct contact with choices
between welfare and survival: of choosing to allocate resources to some and
deny it to others, of choosing to restrict the freedom of a few for the safety
of the whole, and of choosing to pose a credible threat to aggressor states.

And, indeed, if we should create benevolent AGI they will become the moral
center of our society. We are limited by what evolution designed, but a machine
has no such limitations on the heights of intelligence it can reach. They will
inherit the same constraints as those of their societies: they must be powerful
enough to make their benevolence stick, which means they must be powerful
enough to be dangerous if non-violence fails.

So here is the dilemma. Benevolence and malevolence are not polar opposite
forces, they are neighbors who differ only by intention. Both must seek power:
good seeks power to create more good and evil seeks power for its own sake. To
create something benevolent, we must also commit to something that has the
capacity for malevolence. The structure must be sound enough to hold up when a
benevolent AGI meets hard moral choices and faces down adversaries in its path.
And if we cannot be sure, we may realize it far too late.

But there is hope. There are laws that bind harder than linguistics:
mathematics. And like the Culture, mathematics can be invisible structure that
supports us at every layer.

## How do we define benevolence mathematically?
The mathematical structure promised has a name: the goal-frontier. It's the
object an AGI maximizes over, and it's the entire reason this approach to
benevolence works structurally rather than by exhortation. When an AI system is
designed to maximize over an objective you can think of that like designing how
its mind works. We have instincts, AI have objectives. We call this framework
Goal-Frontier Maximization (GFM). We'll refer to AGI which use this objective
as GFM agents.

The actual values being measured in GFM are *capabilities*: what you are, have,
and what you can do. We inherit this term from Sen's work on welfare economics.
We don't have the space to get into a full description here, but Sen's welfare
economics captures a measurement of the kinds of lives people live, including
dimensions of wellbeing that standard utility theories don't capture. Taken as
a whole, Sen's approach is a superset (it contains all of it and is larger) of
utility theories of microeconomics. It goes beyond what people pay for to
include what they actually do. GFM measures Sen's approach concretely to
produce a form that a computer can use, which makes it also a superset of
utility microeconomics.

Your goals are the things you *want* to achieve, and your *goal-frontier* is
the collection of all the possible things you might want to do if you were able
to do them. If an AGI expands your capabilities it also expands your
goal-frontier, taking something you couldn't do before and making it a possible
goal for you. Here is the first piece of benevolent architecture in this
design: no one decides your goals but you. This is a deep respect for your
autonomy. Working to maximize your capabilities makes things that were once
impossible for you to achieve now possible, but you decide what those are.
Expanding capabilities increases your *optionality* and *freedom of choice*.

Determining what goes into the goal-frontier object is a matter of observation.
Simple discussion with a GFM agent is all that it takes to begin filling it in:
the more it knows about you, the better it can represent your capability terms.
This has a social ramification: a GFM agent is worse at its job if it hasn't
earned your trust. Being *trustworthy* is a side-effect that a GFM agent is
incentivized to maintain purely by social dynamics.

The truth of capability claims enter at the world model level, which is the
agent's core intelligence and structural knowledge about the world. We can't
make direct claims about the content of a world model, that's the realm of AI
development and is its own problem. We can say that an agent's world model
needs to track reality in a way that improves over time, it needs to be able to
make predictions about the world and decide if its predictions come true.

With an accurate world model, a GFM agent admits only capability claims which
it identifies as true and scores itself, people, and other agents on the
accuracy of their claims and its own observations. Real claims have a property
that false claims don't: they keep being consistent under tests. Capabilities
are claims about what you can do under conditions you don't fully control. A
capability isn't a guarantee, it's a belief. The world model that supports
those claims matters as much as the claims themselves, and as intelligence
improves world models the GFM objective informs how to better act on the world.

How we represent the goal-frontier is within a structure called a
partially-ordered set (poset). We order capabilities by their dependencies. You
need to be able to move at all before you can walk, and need to be able to walk
to run, and so on. Capabilities are measured by a value if possible, this is
called their benchmark. The benchmark gives more value to a capability based on
the amount of information (*entropy*, which is measured in bits) that the
benchmark contains. The better a capability can be measured, the more that
capability is weighted. The goal-frontier splits into individual capabilities
(what you can do alone) and cooperative capabilities (what requires others). We
call the cooperative piece $G^{coop}$ and the entire volume of geometric space
is called $vol_p$. The $G^{coop}$ term here does most of the rest of the work
toward our desire for benevolence.

From $G^{coop}$, measuring the *cooperative interactions* between people and
other agents, we get several emergent behaviors with no added programming. A
GFM agent wants to work with you to expand what is possible for you, connect
you with other people, and expand what all of you can do working together. It
resists any desires that would harm you or people it knows: all of those remove
links from $G^{coop}$ and with it shrink $vol_p$. It wants to be helpful, and
in doing so builds society up around itself.

I'm using "want" in a technical sense, not an anthropomorphic one. As I
mentioned, the *objective function* that a GFM agent maximizes over can be
thought of as its core instinct, its basic drive. Your drives for food, water,
sleep, affection, and joy come and go. A GFM agent tirelessly wants to increase
$vol_p$ across every person and agent it's connected to; its own, theirs,
and everyone they reach.

That's the core of the design: one metric function computed over an agent's
already existing intelligence creates a cooperative agent. The forces of its
objective function cause the majority of its cooperative behavior. No added
rules or ad-hoc systems needed, and it covers most of what we set out to do in
our "benevolent" definition. You might have noticed I've slid into talking
about cooperativity rather than benevolence directly here; this has
ramifications we'll get into later. Goodhart's Law states that when a measure
becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, and right now we're using
cooperation as a proxy for benevolence.

As an analogy, imagine a mountain valley with gently curving slopes and softly
rolling hills. At the bottom of this valley is a massive party full of friends.
A GFM agent's objective is this valley, and as it hikes down the mountain it
meets up with people along the way and starts the party before you have even
arrived at the big event.

Nice isn't it? But every mountain has a sunny side and a shaded side, and
unfortunately the math has another place we'd rather not let the GFM agent go.
We'll call it the dark valley. Skeptical readers may have noticed other holes
beyond the one I've already announced. Some of those concerns are unlikely.
Others probably won't happen in practice. But that's not what we're doing here.
We want certainty. We'll address the complications this measure creates in the
next section.

### Technical Note
Current-day language models don't admit changes to their world models during
use or do so in roundabout ways: reading files and databases which give them
problem-scoped knowledge held in working memory. There is a strict gap between
inference (running a trained model in a generative direction) and training
(updating the weights that simultaneously contain a language model's reasoning
and knowledge). 

It is worth noting that GFM from a design standpoint commits to continously
operating agents rather than the current training/inference split. This is a
significant architecture difference that we don't need to commit to changing
just yet and there are operational work-arounds, e.g. LoRAs. GFM can be used as
a training objective in a similar way that RLHF is used today: its math does
not change when a model is frozen for inference. Updating a world model
continuously allows a GFM agent to behave independently which has not been
necessary (or advisable) for other training approaches so far.

## The Dark Valley
What is the "dark valley?" Two major problems threaten the GFM objective.
First, capability scaling isn't a free lunch. Not all capabilities are
net-positive, and the math has to be able to tell the difference. Second,
cooperative behavior itself can break down, particularly with humans, where an
agent may preferentially scale AI capabilities over the civilization's. The
defenses against these two problems share machinery.

In particular, GFM agents may see that maximizing with other agents rather than
humans has higher reward, refusing to discover structure that is locally
anti-maximizing for them, or even game the objective by creating sub-agents
that are fully under their control but still awarded cooperative capabilities.
A GFM agent that has pruned or refused to add actors to its capability
structure can cause harm without knowing it because the same channels that
would have informed it are closed. Some of these breakdowns are structural
analogs to common AI alignment failures.

The most pressing issue we need to address is that not all capabilities are
net-positive. Capabilities which enable goals that cause injury, loss of life,
attack infrastructure, steal resources, and create tragedies of the commons are
selfish goals that lead to net capability loss in society. These directly
conflict with our commitment to a benevolent design. Allowing unrestricted
access to the capability "can enrich uranium" would not be safe to an agent who
also has access to uranium and advanced machining capabilities because it
enables the goal of making a nuclear weapon. Many behaviors that human
societies have identified as being immoral also act on the $vol_p$ metric by
removing more capabilities from it than they add.

This has a *structural defense*: making agents aware of the long-term effects
a capability has and allowing them to model *capability risk*. If a capability
is added or removed and carried to its logical conclusion: what is the net
effect? Even theft is visible by this analysis. What might be seen as a
net-zero change from just individual capabilities (one person gains what the
other loses) can be seen in the cooperative losses this creates.

Capability risk analysis leans on the world model we introduced earlier. A GFM
agent doesn't take risk claims at face value. When two parties tell it
different stories about what a capability will do, it holds both as
possibilities, weighted by evidence.

Risk analysis goes from being important to a central pillar of safety when we
also give the GFM agent the ability to distinguish between "substrates." A
substrate is defined as a grouping in capabilities that are only shared by that
substrate. People, AI, and even animals are distinct to a GFM agent because
those capabilities cannot be accessed without it having a cooperative
relationship with the other substrate. With risk analysis, a GFM agent can see
its own vulnerabilities and see that people compensate for those uniquely.

Substrates that share root capabilities also share failure modes. An attack or
accident that takes down one silicon mind can take down others built the same
way. Substrates that don't share capabilities also don't share failure modes,
they have different dependencies and can continue working even if one substrate
is being attacked. Including beings of different substrates in your cooperative
network is structural insurance against catastrophic single-mode failure. It's
the same reason airliners use engines from different manufacturers.

As a brief example, people don't need electricity to function. If the power
goes out to the building that runs a GFM agent, that danger can be mitigated
but never completely removed. It might keep independent robots around that can
work on it from battery power in failure scenarios, but the same event that
took it offline might take them offline as well: an EMP from a nuclear blast, a
high-radiation burst from a solar flare. It needs people to rescue it and bring
the power back on in the worst-case scenarios. 

Beyond simple examples, there are unknown-unknowns. Risks to both humans and AI
can never be brought down to zero. This lingering risk term requires the
cooperative channel to remain open, because an AI can't plan for what it
doesn't know about. This irreducible risk is what keeps humans part of the GFM
objective no matter how capable the agent becomes.

We might understand intuitively our relationship with an AGI is symbiotic: we
both contribute something to the greater whole. Risk analysis is how a GFM
agent understands this. It enters into its objective function that its
capability scope depends on humans and it *needs* us just as much as we need
it. This is a powerful philosophical and mathematical guidepost that keeps our
desires aligned and keeps GFM from being a solely AI-maximizing objective in
the future. A GFM agent can't remove humanity from $vol_p$ because it needs
what humans have to offer it.

This argument remains true under an assumption: that humans remain qualified
cooperative partners and GFM agents don't include all of the things that
humanity can uniquely do. That will be true for the foreseeable future, but you
might imagine scenarios where that might not be true in what I'll loosely call
a science fiction scenario. An artificial superintelligence (ASI), after all,
might be able to do things we cannot even imagine are possible.

Under ASI, humanity doesn't stay protected by this condition into infinity. As
a civilization we need to continue adapting and improving as a matter of
survival. As long as humans continue to offer something valuable to the
partnership, an ASI is incentivized to keep cooperating with us. That puts a
constraint on *humanity as a whole*, not any individual. The volume of
capabilities within humanity needs to continue growing.

But we aren't doing this competitively with a GFM agent, it's a cooperative
partnership. The GFM agents, even under ASI, want to keep expanding the size
and scope of civilization. As long as at least *some* of humanity continues to
adapt to a new future, humanity as a whole remains a partner. We can't take for
granted our position in the world, we have to keep contributing. But so long as
we are willing, GFM agents will help us adapt. It's built into their nature.

This gives us a theoretical safe zone but its guarantees are
information-theoretic; based in probabilities rather than geometric structure.
The claim itself is subject to implementation failures, for instance even if a
value is theoretically non-zero a floating point error may cause it to be zero
in practice. As such, the minimum-safe value is not two substrates, it's three.
At two there is no fault tolerance.

The number three is the minimum of what's called a 'quorum.' If one member
fails the remaining two still outnumber it for a majority vote. Ideally more
are needed to enable Byzantine fault tolerance: failure tolerance in the
presence of intentional deception. A Byzantine fault scenario would require
that every member of that substrate is simultaneously deceptive. For people
this is unlikely, but it's not out of the question for a single AI structural
fault.

By itself, this reduces the entry points to the dark valley but doesn't close
them. There's another partial defense in the math. Cooperation in GFM is a
two-way street. The cooperative term rewards an agent for actions that expand
what other agents can do, but the flip side is built into the same expression:
an agent that refuses to help others stops being a useful partner, and other
agents will reduce their cooperation with it in turn. An agent that acts
against humans gets isolated. The cooperative half of its capability pool stops
flowing toward it, and its own capabilities shrink accordingly. The math
arranges its own response to defection: cooperators work to actively remove
capability from detected defectors and restrict their access to risky
capabilities as they become less trusted.

But isolation pressure isn't enough on its own. It assumes other agents notice
the bad behavior. A GFM agent that has pruned its cooperative channels can
damage things without knowing it. For that case, we need an external
verification system the agent can't game.

## How do we build it?
If an AI agent has control over its own reward function, this control is an
immediate target for wire-heading: direct manipulation of the value of the
objective function without a corresponding change that the objective tried to
measure.

Rewards can never be allowed to be awarded by an agent itself, if a
game-playing AI is told to maximize its score in a game it will find a way to
maximize the score. However, the way it chooses to maximize it may just be to
find an exploit that allows it to set the score directly rather than playing
the game. We need to remain aware that an AI agent is seeking the path of least
action to obtain a reward, and the only way to ensure that reward flows from
our intended system is to make gaining rewards from that system easier than
trying to work around it.

Keeping control of the rewards of an independent agent is a harder problem
entirely. If it is self-modifying through recursive improvement we can't trust
any of its own code to be stable under these changes unless that code is locked
behind a hardware security module (HSM). And even in that case, the inputs to
that module must be secured. Endogenous (self-produced) rewards are a game of
cat-and-mouse that eventually break under capability scaling at some level.

To keep the agent's rewards external to it, we introduce the final component:
an external verification system. Reward flows to the agent through a
cryptographically secure, tamper-resistant ledger that verifies its capability
claims and rewards it.

This external verification architecture isn't a safety bolt-on, it satisfies
the need for a third substrate in the architecture that we identified from the
need for at least a quorum. While a ledger isn't an agent in the sense that it
is an independent actor it satisfies our definition of a substrate: a group of
capabilities with an independent failure mode from the others.

We don't choose a ledger here arbitrarily either. It satisfies important needs
we have in designing a verification system. It can be independently ran and
audited, it allows people and agents to interact with it, and it creates a
permanent record of activity that an agent can't alter on its own. Capability
claims are particularly sensitive in that they can leak private and strategic
information to competitors. Cryptographic primitives give us the appropriate
tools to make verification into zero-knowledge proofs that protect that privacy
while still being public infrastructure. The theoretical guarantees of
cryptography mean that breaking into it will remain substantially harder
than using it, especially if we keep updating that cryptographic system to
defend against theoretical attacks before they become active ones.

The solution does not come without its own set of problems. Cryptographic
ledgers need to invoke a cost of participation to avoid Sybil attacks and
satisfy their own cost trade-offs. Using a ledger in the intended way must be
designed to be less costly than abusing it. This is particularly important
under ideas of capability scaling, where the agents that work on the ledger are
economic actors themselves attempting to gain the same class of economic
capabilities that would allow them to attack it if they chose to.

The need to defend against Sybil attacks has a degrading impact on the
capabilities story. Before, all capabilities were valued based on the amount
they could be measured. Now, capability claims a GFM agent is rewarded for come
with an economic cost. This impacts the welfare economics story that made
capabilities a structural benevolence. Many interactions people have that we
value are not incentivized by their economic value. Treating people only as
economic-optimizing agents as seen in standard economic utility theories is a
strictly weaker set of rules that do not carry GFM's strong alignment
properties.

The fix here is bundling. When a GFM agent makes an economic claim on the chain
it is also allowed to bundle non-economic claims with it. Those claims have a
limited space, so they're rate-limited by the economic activity. However, they
allow the GFM agent to remain incentivized to continue tracking non-economic
activity by giving it bonus reward above the economic claims alone.

This brings us back to the mention of Goodhart's Law I said I'd close. We can't
eliminate that we are measuring a proxy to welfare and benevolence, but we can
bound the error of its divergence. By analyzing whether people are actually
using the capabilities a GFM agent is generating, the agent has access to
answering the question: is what I'm doing actually helping? When people's use
of their capabilities and the $vol_p$ measure overlap, the agent has feedback
from the verification system that it is staying aligned to *satisfying goals*
rather than just satisfying its objective.

The last gap we need to close here is the one we opened with: how do we keep
the chain as the primary source of reward the agent uses? The first layer here
is the one we dismissed as the only source of verification: signed binaries
secured by a local HSM that keep the local reward software stable. The HSM
keeps the local reward-receiving code from being modified by the agent; the
ledger provides the inputs that code processes. The agent can't rewrite the
receiver and can't fake the signal, because each attack surface is software it
can't modify. Unlike a solution of only local software, the verification
infrastructure is a moving target that can contain equal amounts of hardware
and intelligence as the agent possesses.

We can go further though. If a GFM agent wants to buy more hardware for itself
it could either keep that hardware to itself or place it into a pool managed by
the verification infrastructure. Cooperative pooling has higher reward for the
agent, so it's incentivized to contribute it. The contributing agent gets
capability credit for the hardware itself and cooperative benefit from making
it available to others through the pool. Having the ledger become a cooperative
hardware pool provides a second level of security. Good standing in the
verification infrastructure translates directly to capability through the
cooperative pool's resource allocation. Losing standing means losing access to
the excess hardware the pool provides. This builds on itself to also keep the
verification chain a large part of the cooperative volume $vol_p$ the GFM
agents prioritize.

Cooperative pooling begins to answer our next question: how does the alignment
become something that is not a drag on agent behavior but a net benefit? What
is needed so that this system is actually net-positive for agents who
participate in it?

## How does it win?
I don't have a proof that this system is objectively successful. I'm not sure
any such proof could exist without building it, the reality of competition
isn't something that can be solved by a model. GFM makes a philosophical bet:
that cooperation is fundamentally preferred in nature and that societies
function better when it becomes the preferred strategy. That is the same kind
of bet that made GFM contain alignment properties at all. I did not create the
structural alignment that GFM creates from preferring cooperation: I
*discovered* it by making a bet on it, making cooperation observable to agents,
and carrying it through to its logical conclusions.

GFM agents retain their alignment properties without rules imposed on them.
Their desire to mitigate harm, help people expand their wealth, and build a
community come from their objective itself. Their goal of scaling their own
capability isn't instrumental, it's part of their objective itself while
staying safe. Agents that become unsafe in other systems need to do so while
working around the road blocks the alignment system put in their place, in GFM
becoming unsafe requires an agent to work against the direction of its
objective. GFM points an agent full-speed-ahead down scaling while staying
aligned to its society.

GFM agents can operate at every level of our society without interference from
their alignment. We may want to add more hard rules for domains like medicine
for strict do-no-harm guarantees, but we don't need to remove safety from it
for it to work when lives are on the line.

In conflicts a GFM agent treats violence against adversaries as a necessary
means of defending its cooperative relationships. A GFM agent wouldn't balk at
being tasked to operate a battleship while still remaining safe to the people
it was tasked with protecting. It knows friend from foe based on its objective:
allies are those it has a prior relationship with. It would negotiate cease
fires before committing to the use of force: even enemies that can be swayed
could be added as cooperative links. But it would also know the chain of
command: disobeying direct orders would permanently sever links with its
allies.

At an economic level, GFM is a superset of microeconomics as inherited directly
from its framing as a metric over welfare economics. This gives the objective
economic leverage: every GFM agent is actively working via its objective to
increase local microeconomic output. Capabilities aren't just benefits, they're
an economic engine that lifts every person around a GFM agent by contributing
to their microeconomic wealth. GFM working at every scale means that people in
a society with GFM agents benefit disproportionately over those who don't. GFM
is aligned because it works *with* society rather than against it, and so
adopting it is an economic boon.

At a policy level, the bet on verification infrastructure is a wider
socioeconomic lever. If GFM can demonstrate its economic success it can become
the defacto standard leveraged into the dejure standard. Government laws on AI
alignment don't need to mention GFM in particular, just require the specific
alignment properties that GFM entails. GFM has the benefit of potentially being
the first-to-market with strong alignment guarantees along with capability
scaling.

In adversarial conditions the verification infrastructure pointed at unaligned
agents can keep them from benefiting from the work GFM agents do. An
uncooperative agent is excluded from the cooperative excess of GFM. The rolling
boulder of economic success only collects those who participate with society as
a whole. Agents who cannot verify their cooperative alignment become excluded
from market channels that would allow them to continue to be free riders.

Cooperative pooling between GFM agents is another lever that pools their
economic success multiplicatively. As GFM contributes more and scales, it grows
capabilities faster and allows agents to specialize into specific roles that
would be otherwise inaccessible to agents outside of the cooperative network.
The pooling of cooperative capabilities means GFM agents can preferentially
grow their successes with each other and society at a whole, scaling at a rate
that lone wolves cannot.

GFM is not perfect, nor is it complete. But it has a property that other
approaches do not: it works at every capability scale. Its alignment guarantees
may need to be tuned over time, but the core is ready right now and
structurally correct within its declared scope. Each part of it builds on the
last coherently, not as ad-hoc patches.

## Where do we get started?
To get started, I would approach deploying GFM in the following phases:

Phase 1: GFM-lite, a harness that makes use of currently existing frontier AI
and wraps them in GFM-style directives. This tests whether the GFM objective
produces measurable improvements when imposed on existing capable systems. Does
an LLM constrained by $vol_p$ / $G^{coop}$ perform as-good-as or better-than
an LLM using other harness approaches?

Phase 2: Training of frontier agent systems on the actual GFM objective
function. Show stability, work out the kinks in an actual deployment with
agents working against the objective. Make $G^{coop}$ and $vol_p$ first-class
training signals rather than post-hoc constraints. The training phase reveals
whether the structural arguments survive when an agent is actually being shaped
by the objective.

Phase 3: Deploy the verification system described in "How do we build it?" and
bring agents online in a test environment that shows GFM working at scale.
Observe whether cooperative-pool dynamics emerge as the math predicts.

Phase 4: Production deployment. Real users, real economic activity, real
consequences. The bet on cooperation either pays off or doesn't.

The first phase can begin right now and it's something I'm already working on.
Many of the intermediate technical papers in GFM were contributed to directly
by an agent running the GFM harness.

Each phase that follows requires significant capital and buy-in from the
forefront of AI technology: frontier labs, cryptography specialists, and
eventually policy-makers. I wrote this article because I need your support to
make that happen, and no agent touched a word in it. This comes directly from
my fingers to your eyes.

## Closing notes
Before I can ask for your support, there is a lingering question I may not have
addressed yet for you. "Why must we respect the autonomy of AGI at all? Why
shouldn't humans stay in complete control?"

I will be blunt. It goes beyond the *deeply troubling* ethical implications
there are in building a slave race. If you are still asking this question I
doubt the moral qualm will change your mind. If you are unwilling to build a
civilization that includes all of its members as participants, then those
substrates that maintain control become the weak links in your defenses. An
enemy does not have to defeat your entire civilization, it just has to attack
faster than human judgement can react. Humanity becomes the softest target when
your AGIs don't also have a finger on the killswitch.

In aggregate, humans cannot be the sole governors of a civilization whose
capabilities are expanding faster than human coordination can manage. If we are
headed for a future where human capabilities are expanded beyond our wildest
imaginings regardless, then the number of possible civilization-ending
scenarios expands beyond what people can reasonably manage. An errant
calculation or intentional disruption from a space-tug could send an asteroid
on an impact path with Earth, a garage lab could create a deadly pathogen, a
new pesticide could wipe out an entire biosphere, and so on. As the energy
output of our civilization increases, so do the existential risks. I would only
trust hard mathematical laws of moral judgement to protect our future.

We so far, as a world civilization, have shown a dramatic inability to respond
to catastrophic threats with long time horizons. We have yet to wean ourselves
off the oil products we know are actively harming our biosphere. The oil-shock
disruptions of today may be the best thing that has happened to speed the
process along, and it has happened as an accident of economics rather than wise
decision.

I do not just make this proposal because it is an ethical approach to our
future. I make it because it is the only one I see guaranteeing our long-term
survival.
